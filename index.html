Part 2: Simulation

Changing the spring constant ks modifies how flexible the cloth is; at a lower spring constant (10 N/m), the cloth droops more and changes more quickly. A higher spring constant (10,000 N/m) makes the cloth hold its structure more tightly and therefore change more slowly, resulting in a cloth similar to the structure it began as.

Changing the density controls how much mass the cloth has; for a lower density, the cloth appears more lightweight or sheer, where the cloth moves more rapidly and responsively. At a higher density, the cloth seems to be heavier and slower to react to change as it drops.

Changing the damping parameter changes how many oscillations the cloth undergoes and how quickly it settles to its final resting position. Increasing damping made the cloth settle more quickly, while lowering it made the cloth much more flexible, oscillating for a while before settling.

Part 3: Handling Collisions with Other Objects

For sphere collisions, we first determine whether a point mass has intersected the sphere by checking whether its position is inside the sphere. If it has, we find the point where the movement intersects the sphere's surface. We then calculate a correction vector that moves the point mass to the sphere's surface, accounting for the collision friction so that the cloth does not move as much after colliding with the sphere.

For plane collisions, we similarly find the point where the point mass's movement intersects the plane. We then calculate a correction vector that moves the point mass to the plane's surface. We apply a small surface offset along the plane normal to ensure the point mass is slightly above the surface and does not remain intersecting it. Finally, we scale the correction by the friction parameter.

Part 4: Handling Self-Collisions

Cloth with self-collision awareness implemented

To implement self-collisions efficiently, we use spatial hashing. We divide the cloth's space into discrete 3D bounding boxes and hash each point mass's position to determine which box it belongs to. When checking for collisions, we look up the point masses in the same spatial box, excluding the current point mass. We then check whether any of these candidate point masses are too close to the current point mass. If a collision is detected, we calculate a correction vector that separates the two point masses, maintaining a minimum distance of twice the cloth's thickness.

When we vary density, we can see that the high density cloth folds on itself quickly, allowing stretching and shrinking. On the other hand, low density cloth appears more airy and resists deformation more.

With a high spring constant, the cloth resists stretching and deformation, resulting in larger and fewer folds. With a low spring constant, the cloth folds more easily and quickly because it offers less resistance to deformation.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program consists of a vertex shader and a fragment shader. The vertex shader transforms vertex positions into screen space and passes other information, such as normals, texture coordinates, and positions, to the fragment shader. The fragment shader uses this interpolated information to calculate the final color of each fragment on the mesh's triangles.

Blinn-Phong shading combines three components: ambient, diffuse, and specular lighting. Ambient lighting approximates indirect light and provides a baseline amount of illumination across the surface. Diffuse lighting depends on the angle between the surface normal and the direction of the light, making surfaces facing the light appear brighter. It models matte surfaces. Specular lighting creates shiny highlights and depends on the relationship between the light direction and the viewing direction.

When using bump mapping, the height map is used to modify the surface normal in lighting calculations, creating the illusion of texture on the surface. However, the actual geometry of the sphere does not change, so its silhouette and edges remain smooth.

Changing the mesh coarseness has little effect on bump mapping because the geometry remains unchanged and the height map is sampled per fragment to modify the normals used in lighting calculations. Displacement mapping, however, changes the actual geometry by moving vertices according to the height map. With a coarser mesh, there are fewer vertices to capture the height variations, so fine details are lost and the sphere's silhouette appears less detailed. A finer mesh has more vertices and can represent these details more accurately.
